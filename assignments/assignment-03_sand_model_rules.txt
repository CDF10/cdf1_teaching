2. GRAIN-SCALE RULES (what each particle does)
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
The model is a 3D soft-sphere Discrete Element Method (DEM): every grain is a
sphere with position x, velocity v, angular velocity w, radius R, mass m.
Grains may overlap very slightly; the overlap delta produces the contact
force (spring + damper).                                              [STD]

2.1 Motion (Newton's laws)                                            [STD]
      m  dv/dt = m g + sum(F_contact) + F_drag
      I  dw/dt = sum(r x F_t) + sum(M_roll)          I = 2/5 m R^2
    Integration: velocity Verlet.
    Time step: dt <= 0.2 * T_Rayleigh,
      T_Rayleigh = pi R sqrt(rho/G) / (0.1631 nu + 0.8766)
    (G shear modulus, nu Poisson ratio). Because real quartz stiffness gives
    tiny time steps, E is reduced to ~1e7 Pa; the literature shows the angle
    of repose is NOT sensitive to Young's modulus, density, Poisson ratio or
    the damping coefficient, so this is allowed.                      [LIT 7]

2.2 Normal contact force - Hertz law                                  [STD]
      R* = R1 R2/(R1+R2)      m* = m1 m2/(m1+m2)
      E* = E / (2 (1 - nu^2))   (same material)
      F_n = 4/3 E* sqrt(R*) delta^(3/2)            (repulsive spring)
    Damping from the coefficient of restitution e:
      beta = ln(e) / sqrt(ln(e)^2 + pi^2)
      S_n  = 2 E* sqrt(R* delta)
      F_nd = -2 sqrt(5/6) beta sqrt(S_n m*) v_n    (v_n normal rel. speed)
    => slow grains stick, fast grains bounce a little (e ~ 0.3 - 0.5).

2.3 Tangential contact force - Mindlin + Coulomb                      [STD]
      S_t = 8 G* sqrt(R* delta)
      F_t = -S_t * xi_t  (xi_t = accumulated tangential slip of THIS contact,
                          stored as long as the contact exists = static
                          friction memory)
      |F_t| <= mu_s * |F_n|   (Coulomb limit; if exceeded the grain slides)
    Sliding friction mu_s ~ 0.5 for quartz sand.
    The angle of repose increases with sliding friction.               [LIT 6,7]

2.4 Rolling resistance (replaces grain shape)                    [LIT 8, STD]
    Real sand grains are angular, not round. Perfect spheres roll away and
    give piles that are far too flat. A rolling resistance torque mimics the
    shape (elastic-plastic spring-dashpot type, Ai et al. 2011):
      k_r     = 2.25 k_n mu_r^2 R*^2
      M_r     = -k_r * theta_r          (spring on relative rolling angle)
      |M_r|  <= mu_r R* |F_n|           (plastic limit)
    mu_r ~ 0.1 - 0.3, calibrated so a test pile reaches ~34 deg.
    The angle of repose increases strongly with rolling friction.      [LIT 6,7]
    Rough, angular sand piles steeper than smooth rounded sand.         [LIT 1]

2.5 Ground and walls                                                  [STD]
    Same contact laws against planes and Rhino meshes (sphere-triangle
    contact). A rough ground (higher mu) holds the bottom grains in place.

2.6 Air drag (needed for realistic drop height)                 [LIT 13, STD]
      Re  = rho_air |v| d / mu_air
      C_d = 24/Re (1 + 0.15 Re^0.687)    (Schiller-Naumann, Re < 1000)
      F_drag = -1/2 rho_air C_d (pi d^2/4) |v| v
    Terminal velocity of a 0.3 mm quartz grain in air: about 2.3 m/s
    (our own calculation with this formula, Re ~ 47; to be checked).
    Compare: free fall without air from 1 m gives 4.4 m/s.
    => above roughly 0.5 - 1 m drop height, fine sand barely gets faster.
    Real sand grains fall a bit slower than spheres (shape factor ~0.7). [LIT 13]

2.7 Grain size distribution                                          [ASM]
    Radii drawn from a lognormal distribution, fine sand d50 ~ 0.2-0.5 mm.
    Real grain numbers are impossible -> "coarse-grained" particles of a
    few mm. Note: larger particles give LOWER angles of repose in DEM, so
    the calibration of mu_r has to be done at the particle size we use. [LIT 7]

2.8 Material parameter table (dry quartz sand)
      grain density rho_p      2650 kg/m^3
      bulk density             ~1500 - 1700 kg/m^3 (packing fraction 0.58-0.64)
      Young's modulus E        1e7 Pa (reduced, see 2.1)
      Poisson ratio nu         0.25
      restitution e            0.3 - 0.5
      sliding friction mu_s    0.5
      rolling friction mu_r    0.1 - 0.3 (calibrated)
      air density / viscosity  1.2 kg/m^3 / 1.8e-5 Pa s
      gravity g                9.81 m/s^2

3. PILE-SCALE LAWS (what the whole heap must do - used to CHECK the model)
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
These laws are not programmed directly. They must COME OUT of the grain
rules in section 2. If they do not, the grain parameters are wrong.

3.1 Angle of repose of dry sand                                     [LIT 1,2]
      dry sand, pile angle  ~ 34 - 35 deg
      crater (hole) angle   ~ 39 deg
    The crater value is close to the "angle of maximum stability".

3.2 Two angles: static vs. dynamic (hysteresis)                [LIT 1,9,10]
    - A slope stays still until it reaches the angle of maximum stability
      theta_max (steepest possible).
    - Then an avalanche starts and the slope relaxes to the lower angle of
      repose theta_r.  theta_max - theta_r ~ a few degrees.
    - Continuous flow gives a smaller angle than slow static building.
    => This is the physical reason for observation 4.1 ("slow -> steeper").

3.3 Flowing layer on the slope - mu(I) rheology              [LIT 11]
    In the moving surface layer the effective friction depends on how fast
    it is sheared:
      I     = gamma_dot d / sqrt(P / rho_p)     (inertial number)
      mu(I) = mu_s + (mu_2 - mu_s) / (I0 / I + 1)
      slope angle of flowing layer: tan(theta) = mu(I)
    Values measured for glass beads: mu_s = tan(20.9 deg),
    mu_2 = tan(32.76 deg), I0 = 0.279 (sand values differ, must be fitted).
    mu_s corresponds to the quasi-static angle of repose.
    Limitation: mu(I) does not capture hysteresis.                    [LIT 11]
    Used for: (a) checking the DEM, (b) a fast continuum model later.

3.4 Pile size                                                        [LIT 12]
    Very small piles have a LOWER angle; the angle rises with the number
    of grains and then settles to a constant value.
    => our test piles must be large enough (thousands of grains) before we
       measure the angle.

3.5 Shape of the cone (not a perfect cone!)                   [LIT 4,5,14,15]
    a) Flanks: nearly straight at theta_r, but often slightly CONVEX. [LIT 15]
    b) Foot / tail: at the bottom the slope runs out with a logarithmic
       correction (concave tail), not a sharp edge.             [LIT 4,14]
    c) Top: the tip is ROUNDED, not sharp. The rounding of the top of a
       heap is discussed in Herrmann 1999.                           [LIT 14]
    d) The top position wanders sideways while the pile grows (avalanches
       left/right).                                                  [LIT 16]
    e) Under the apex the pressure on the ground has a DIP (minimum), it is
       not maximal under the highest point.                          [LIT 4,5]

3.6 Apex sharpness rule (how "spitz" the top is)                    [ASM]
    No closed literature formula found. Our model rule:
      The top is flat/rounded over the zone where grains land and are still
      moving. Rounding radius at the top
        r_top ~ r_stream + r_bounce
        r_stream = radius of the falling stream where it hits the pile
        r_bounce = typical horizontal jump after impact ~ (e v_imp)^2 / g
                   (ballistic jump, v_imp = impact speed from 2.6)
      Consequences:
        - low drop height, narrow stream -> small r_top -> SHARP apex
        - high drop height -> faster impact -> bigger jumps -> ROUND apex
        - wide stream / moving source -> flat top (ridge with crest, like
          crest_width in sand_ridge_along_curve)
      Must be checked with a DEM parameter study and a real experiment.

3.7 Pour rate from a funnel / hopper - Beverloo law               [LIT 3]
      W = C rho_b sqrt(g) (D - k d)^(5/2)       [kg/s]
        D outlet diameter, d grain diameter, rho_b bulk density
        C ~ 0.5 - 0.7, k ~ 1.2 - 3 (k ~ 2.9 reported for sand)
      valid when D is large enough to avoid jamming and grains > ~0.5 mm.
      2D / slit outlet: exponent 3/2 instead of 5/2.
    Used to set a REALISTIC flow rate in the emitter.

4. OUR OBSERVATIONS AND HOW THE MODEL EXPLAINS THEM
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
4.1 "The lower the speed, the higher and steeper the cone"           [OBS]
    Explanation: slow arrival -> grains stop before the next arrive -> slope
    can build up close to theta_max (3.2). Fast pour -> continuous flowing
    layer -> slope follows mu(I) at the lower dynamic angle (3.3).
    Literature confirms discharge rate changes the angle of repose.    [LIT 2]
    Model input: emitter flow rate Q (kg/s), Beverloo (3.7).

4.2 "Frequency of hitting the bottom"                          [OBS, ASM]
      impact frequency f = Q / m_grain
      settling time  t_s ~ time a grain needs to stop after impact
      ratio Pi = f * t_s
        Pi << 1 : grains arrive one by one -> quasi-static, steep cone
        Pi >> 1 : grains disturb each other -> flowing surface, flatter cone
    The model measures Pi in every run and outputs it with the result.

4.3 "The nearer, the steeper the cone"                          [OBS, ASM]
    Small drop height h -> low impact speed v_imp = sqrt(2 g h) (with drag:
    2.6) -> little energy -> grains stop where they land -> steep cone,
    sharp apex (3.6).

4.4 "Higher distance -> more energy and broader spread"         [OBS, ASM]
    Impact energy E = 1/2 m v_imp^2 grows with h until terminal velocity
    (2.6) is reached. More energy -> grains bounce and roll further,
    surface layer is "fluidized" -> flatter flank, wider base, rounder top,
    sometimes a small crater at the impact point. The stream itself also
    widens while falling (collisions between grains in the air).
    Supporting literature: more potential energy -> more friction overcome
    -> lower final angle (shown for column collapse, not yet for pouring).
    No study found that isolates drop height for pouring -> we must test
    this ourselves (section 5).

6.2 HOW THE PHYSICS REACHES EVERY GEOMETRY
   Two levels, both obeying the same laws:
   a) EXACT: grain simulation (DEM). The shape is the result of grains
      colliding -> all grain rules hold automatically. Slow (minutes).
   b) FAST: SandField (height field) driven by a SandLaw:
        - poured mass / bulk density = sand volume (mass conservation)
        - sand lands on a disk of radius
             r = outlet_diameter/2 + (e * v_impact)^2 / g      (rule 3.6)
          -> wide stream or high drop = round / flat tip
        - avalanche: sand slides wherever a slope is steeper than
             angle = 34 deg + b ln(v_impact / v_ref) + c ln(Q / Q_ref)
          clamped to [20, 39] deg (3.1, 3.2, 4.1, 4.3, 4.4)
        - v_impact from the fall with air drag (2.6) -> saturates at
          terminal velocity; Q from the outlet diameter (Beverloo 3.7)
        - drop height measured to the CURRENT surface (growing pile)
        - existing geometry is a fixed base the sand cannot erode
      b = -12.2 is PRELIMINARY (from the two grain runs in 1.8); c = 0
      until a flow study is done. SandLaw.from_study(parameter_study(...))
      replaces them with values calibrated by grain simulations.
   => every builder in Part 2 uses one of these two levels, so for any
      shape (points, curves, poured onto meshes) the same laws are met.

6.3 PART 2 - BUILDING AND MODELING (Rhino.Geometry)
   All lengths in Rhino model units (converted to metres inside).
   fast:  sand_pile_at_points, sand_ridge_along_curve_physical, sand_on_mesh
   exact: grain_pour_at_points, grain_pour_along_curve, gh_pour (GH Timer)
   helpers: field_to_mesh, base_from_mesh, grains_to_mesh,
            grains_to_points, grains_to_field_mesh, profile_curve
   Rhino is imported inside try/except, so Part 1 can be tested without
   Rhino. Part 2 was so far only tested with a stand-in for Rhino, not in
   Rhino itself.

6.4 WHERE THE PARAMETER VALUES COME FROM
   a) Literature (measured facts about sand)
      - grain density 2650 kg/m^3 (quartz)                         [LIT 1]
      - angle of repose 34 deg, dry sand                         [LIT 1,2]
      - maximum angle 39 deg (crater angle = steepest slope that
        stands before it avalanches)                              [LIT 2]
      - Beverloo constants C = 0.58, k = 2.9 (k reported for sand) [LIT 3]
      - air density 1.2 kg/m^3, air viscosity 1.8e-5 Pa s (constants)
   b) Typical values used in grain simulations (DEM)          [STD, LIT 6-8]
      - sliding friction 0.5, restitution 0.4, Poisson ratio 0.25
      - rolling friction 0.2 (middle of the usual range 0.1 - 0.3)
      - Young's modulus 1e7 Pa: much softer than real quartz (~7e10 Pa)
        so the simulation runs fast; the angle of repose is hardly
        affected by this                                          [LIT 7]
   c) Calculated from physics
      - impact speed: fall from the drop height incl. air drag on a real
        0.3 mm grain (rule 2.6)
      - landing spread: ballistic jump after impact (rule 3.6)
      - flow rate: from the outlet diameter with the Beverloo law (3.7)
   d) Estimated by us from two test runs                          [ASM]
      - drop-height factor b = -12.2: the grain simulation gave 31.6 deg
        at 3 cm and 22.4 deg at 30 cm drop height; b was chosen so the
        SandLaw formula reproduces this 9.2 deg difference, anchored at
        the literature value 34 deg for a 3 cm drop
      - flow-rate factor c = 0: not measured yet (no effect for now)
      - lower angle limit 20 deg: own safety bound

   No full calibration was done (section 5.1 - 5.3 not run). The exact
   numbers are ESTIMATES. For this project the important point is that
   the effects are contained in the model and based on physics:
      - higher drop -> faster impact -> flatter cone, rounder tip;
        levels off at terminal velocity because of air drag
      - wider outlet -> larger flow rate and wider stream
   Optional later: SandLaw.from_study(parameter_study(...)) replaces the
   estimated b and c with values fitted to grain simulations.

6.5 DIFFERENCES BETWEEN THE GRAIN SIMULATION AND THE SANDFIELD
   Grain simulation (DEM): every grain is a sphere; in tiny time steps
   (~0.00002 s) gravity, air drag and all contacts (push, friction,
   rolling resistance) are computed. Nobody prescribes the pile shape --
   it forms by itself from the collisions.
   SandField: a grid that only stores the HEIGHT of the sand surface.
   Sand is added where the stream lands and slides to the neighbour
   cell wherever a slope is steeper than the allowed angle.

                         grain simulation          SandField
   what is computed      every single grain        only surface height
   source of physics     directly from collisions  from rules (SandLaw)
   speed                 minutes per pile          seconds
   use                   check / explain the       design of any shape
                         physics, animation        in Rhino

   How each physical effect is included in the SandField:
     friction, rolling resistance,  summarised in ONE number:
     grain shape                    the angle of repose
     fall with air drag             computed exactly (impact speed)
     impact energy, bouncing        wider landing zone + flatter angle
                                    at higher impact speed
     outlet size -> flow rate       Beverloo law, computed exactly
     mass conservation              exact
     avalanches on the slope        simplified: sand slides where too steep

   NOT contained in the SandField (only in the grain simulation):
     - individual collisions, rolling and bouncing of grains
     - convex flank and logarithmic tail at the foot (3.5 a, b)
     - timing: impact frequency vs settling time (4.2); the flow rate
       enters only through c, which is 0 for now
     - segregation of large and small grains
     - randomness: equal inputs give identical SandField piles

   Why two levels: simulating every grain of a real pile would take
   days. As in engineering practice, the detailed simulation (or an
   experiment) is used to find simple rules, and the rules are applied at
   large scale. The SandField is this "summary" of the grain physics:
     level 1  grain simulation: the complex physics happens here and shows
              WHY e.g. a higher drop gives a flatter pile
     level 2  SandField: carries these results over to any shape in Rhino

6.6 HOW THE SANDFIELD KNOWS THE ANGLE OF REPOSE
   Before every portion of sand is poured, the SandField asks the
   SandLaw for the allowed angle:
     1. drop height h = outlet height - current sand surface below it
        (the pile grows -> h gets smaller -> the angle changes during
        the pour)
     2. impact speed v from the fall with air drag (physics, rule 2.6)
     3. angle = 34 deg + b ln(v / v_ref) + c ln(Q / Q_ref),
        clamped to 20 - 39 deg
     4. landing radius = outlet_diameter/2 + (e v)^2 / g
   Then the sand is deposited and slides until no slope is steeper than
   this angle.
   Which physical laws are inside, and how:
     explicit   air drag / fall (v), Beverloo (Q), ballistic jump with
                restitution e (landing radius), mass conservation
     implicit   friction, rolling resistance and collisions are NOT
                computed, but their effect is contained in b, which was
                measured with the grain simulation (where they ARE
                computed)
     literature the base value 34 deg comes from measurements of real
                dry sand, not from our grain simulation
   So the SandField contains the grain physics only indirectly, through
   numbers measured with the grain simulation or in the literature.

7. KNOWN LIMITATIONS
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
 - Spheres + rolling resistance instead of real grain shapes.
 - Coarse-grained particles: mass and drag of one model particle differ
   from a real grain -> drop height effects must be scaled carefully.
 - Dry sand only (no moisture / cohesion) - by decision.
 - Apex and drop-height rules (3.6, 4.2-4.4) are our assumptions until
   validated (section 5).
 - SandField reproduces angle, mass, tip rounding and drop-height effect,
   but not the convex flank, the log tail at the foot or the pressure dip
   (3.5 a, b, e); those only appear in the grain simulation.
 - Walls from Rhino meshes are not yet in the grain simulation (only
   planes); in the SandField meshes work as a base.
 - If the outlet is lower than the growing pile it is buried; the
   SandField then uses the steepest allowed angle (39 deg).
